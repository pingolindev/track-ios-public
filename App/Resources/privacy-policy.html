2. Our zero-knowledge commitment

Pingolin is built on a zero-knowledge architecture. All sensitive user data is encrypted on your device, before it reaches our servers, with keys your device generates at random; those keys are themselves sealed under a key derived from your password, which is why changing your password re-seals them rather than re-encrypting your data. Location is the important variation, and it decides who can read you: it is sealed with a key belonging to the circle rather than to you, so every current member of that circle can decrypt it - and because the circle's key chain carries forward, so can anyone who joins later, including for the period before they joined. Your locations, notes, and other sensitive content are stored only as encrypted ciphertext (the limited plaintext metadata we do process is detailed in section 3). We do not hold decryption keys, cannot access your plaintext content, and cannot provide it to any third party - including law enforcement - because we are technically unable to decrypt it. There is one exception in the product itself, and it is named here rather than left to be found: server-side place lookup, if you switch it on, sends us coordinates and search text unencrypted (section 3.5). Separately, anything you write to us directly reaches us in plain text, because we have to read it to answer you (section 3.7). Everything else in this policy assumes the general rule.

This is enforced by the cryptography itself and built into the architecture. The encryption algorithms we use include XChaCha20-Poly1305 for authenticated encryption, Argon2id for key derivation, X25519 for key exchange, Ed25519 for digital signatures, BLAKE3 for hashing, and SRP-6a for zero-knowledge password authentication. We tune the parameters behind these over time as guidance changes; our Security page describes the design in more detail.

3. Data we collect

3.1 Account information

When you create an account, we collect:

  • Email address (plaintext, required for account recovery and communication)
  • A display name, if you set one. This is plaintext and deliberately so - it is the name the other members of your circles see, and encrypting it would defeat its only purpose
  • Encrypted master key. Your password is not stored: signing in uses SRP-6a, which proves you know it without sending it, so there is nothing on our side to steal or to hand over
  • Encrypted key pairs for sharing (X25519) and signing (Ed25519)
  • Key derivation parameters (salt, algorithm settings - not secrets)

3.2 Session and security data

  • Session tokens and refresh tokens
  • Device name and user agent (for session management)
  • Two-factor authentication credentials (TOTP secrets, passkey public keys)

3.3 Anti-abuse checks at registration

Creating an account runs an automated check that the request comes from a real person and, in our mobile apps, from an untampered app. Depending on your platform this sends a token to Cloudflare (Turnstile on the web), to Google (Play Integrity on our standard Android builds) or to Apple (App Attest on iPhone and iPad); all three are listed in section 7. There is a fourth path and it is the one that sends least: our Google-free Android builds present a hardware attestation from the device's secure element, and we verify that certificate chain ourselves against a pinned Google root certificate. Nothing is sent to Google or to anyone else on that path. If the hardware attestation is unavailable, the build falls back to the same Cloudflare Turnstile check, shown inside the app - and that does send a token to Cloudflare. From the chain we read the device's verified-boot state, OS version and patch level to decide whether the request looks legitimate, and we do not keep it after the check. A successful app check replaces the browser check rather than adding to it. These checks tell us only whether the request looks legitimate. They are not used to build a profile of you, and we do not fingerprint your browser or device for any other purpose.

A failed check can block a registration. Where that happens you can ask us to review it by hand at help@support.pingolin.com, the same address Section 4 of our Terms gives for this. The legal basis is legitimate interest (preventing automated abuse).

3.4 IP address and authentication logging

By default, Pingolin does not keep permanent IP address logs associated with your account. IP addresses appear in two places, both bounded in section 6: the session record that keeps you signed in, and the security audit log that records events such as sign-ins and password changes.

IP addresses may be retained for longer if your account is involved in activity that violates our Terms of Service, such as unauthorized access attempts, denial-of-service attacks, or other abuse. We may also be compelled to retain or produce them by a valid legal order.

Optional authentication logging: You may enable detailed authentication logging in your account security settings. When enabled, sign-in attempts (including IP address, device name, and timestamp) are recorded and visible to you. This feature is off by default. Authentication logs are retained only while the feature is enabled and are permanently deleted when you deactivate it. Authentication logs record sign-in events only and do not track product-level activity (such as location updates in Track).

The legal basis for IP retention is legitimate interest (abuse prevention and account security). The legal basis for optional authentication logging is consent (you explicitly enable it).

3.5 Track (Location Sharing)

All location data is end-to-end encrypted. We store:

  • Encrypted location coordinates, altitude, speed, and bearing
  • Encrypted device name and battery level
  • Device type and push notification tokens (plaintext, required for service functionality)
  • User IDs, device IDs and day-level timestamp buckets, in plaintext - for query routing, for working out when a device has gone quiet, and for the retention jobs. Two different times are involved and only one of them is coarse. The event time your device recorded is encrypted, and only a day-level bucket of it is in the clear. The time our servers received the row is stored at millisecond precision, because we use it to order and de-duplicate rows and it forms part of that table's sort key. For live sharing those two times are close together, so we do hold a fine-grained record of when each of your devices uploaded. Treat that as an activity trace we hold, not one we have avoided.
  • A silent instruction to your device to report its location. If a device that has shared before goes quiet for a day or two, our servers send it a content-free push asking it to start again; we look for such devices roughly every fifteen minutes. On iPhone the app takes one position fix and sends it; on Android it restarts the sharing service. It reaches only devices signed in to your account, it carries nothing but the instruction, and it cannot defeat your privacy settings - while a circle is paused or its real-time sharing is off, the app sends that circle nothing. We disclose it because it is our servers causing your phone to report a position without you touching it. Location history itself carries only the day bucket and its precise time is encrypted - but some other Track records do carry a full-precision timestamp in the clear, because the server has to order and expire them: a destination's departure and arrival, a check-in, and a circle notification and its resolution. So we can see when one of those happened, though not where it was or what it said
  • The records behind the features you use - places, place alerts, destinations, check-ins, circle notifications and their delivery records, and your per-circle sharing settings. Their contents are encrypted; what stays in plaintext is the routing and scheduling around them: which member an alert watches, the timezone it is scheduled in, whether you have paused sharing and until when, and the identifiers tying each record to a circle. Two of these deserve naming rather than leaving inside "routing": each arrival at and departure from one of your own places is recorded against you - which place, which person, and when, though not where the place is - and kept for 90 days; and whether a check-in was marked "Safe Arrival" or "All Clear" is stored in the clear, even though the message and the position in it are encrypted
  • Track's own copy of your email address and display name, so it can show you to other members, and - the invitee's email address and the circle's name, in the clear. These are not cleared on the same schedule. The circle's name goes the moment the invitation is accepted, declined or withdrawn, or by a nightly job once it expires. The email address is not cleared at all - the whole invitation is deleted 30 days after it expires, and until then we hold it. If somebody invited you and you have no Pingolin account, that is still data about you: write to us and we will delete it and tell you who invited you, because somebody has to see which circle they are joining before they hold its key
  • A circle activity log: who created, joined, left or was removed from a circle, and when. It records the action and the people, never the circle's contents
  • A record of which pseudonyms belong to you. Location entries are filed under per-circle pseudonyms rather than your user ID, and we keep the mapping between the two - so the pseudonyms compartmentalise your entries, but they do not make them unlinkable to us. We hold this because erasure needs it: without it we could not tell which entries in a shared circle were yours, and deleting your account from the website would leave them behind

Server-side place lookup is the exception to all of that, and it is off unless you turn it on. By default Track works out addresses and place names on your device and sends us nothing. If you enable server-side lookup - a separate setting, which also requires a subscription - then two things reach us unencrypted: the coordinates being looked up, and the text you type when searching for a place. The path is built so neither can be attributed to you: the app spends an anonymous, single-use credit, and the request carries no account, no session and no identifier. We do not log the coordinates or the search text, with one exception: if the lookup service fails, the geocoding server's own error log can record the request that failed, and that request contains them. It carries no account and no session either way. The honest description is that we can see a place was asked about and are not in a position to say whose enquiry it was. The legal basis is contract (it is part of providing the feature you switched on).

3.6 Note (Encrypted Notes)

All note content is end-to-end encrypted. We store:

  • Encrypted note bodies, titles, and notebook names
  • Reminders you set: the time and any repeat rule are in plaintext, so our servers can schedule them; what the reminder says is encrypted, and the notification that reaches your phone carries no note content
  • Encrypted file attachments, stored as encrypted objects in Cloudflare R2, located in Western Europe (see Section 7). The filename and the file type are encrypted too; the size in bytes is not, because we count it against your storage allowance
  • Note and notebook identifiers and sharing membership - for a shared note, the email addresses of the owner and the recipient - in plaintext, for sync routing; the content of what you share stays encrypted
  • Sync timestamps (created and updated times) and version counters, stored in plaintext so the server can order and synchronize your notes across devices
  • Who a note is shared with: the note and notebook identifiers and the email addresses of the owner and the recipient, in plaintext, so we can route it. What is shared stays encrypted, and is encrypted to the recipient rather than to a link

There is no note content we can read. A note you share is encrypted to the person you share it with, and their copy is no more readable to us than yours. There is no public link, no key travelling in a URL, and no way for somebody without an account to open a note. Section 7 of our Terms sets out what sharing means and how to report something shared with you.

3.8 Plaintext metadata and aggregate risk

While all sensitive content is encrypted, we want to be transparent that the plaintext metadata we do store - email addresses, display names, IP addresses, device names, user agents, device types, user IDs, device IDs, day-level timestamp buckets, circle and notebook membership (who you share with), a per-recipient record of how far a circle notification got - whether a push was dispatched to you, whether you were emailed, and whether your device confirmed receipt - the mapping between your location pseudonyms and your account, any support correspondence, and your subscription status and payment reference (a Paddle customer ID, an Apple transaction identifier for an App Store purchase, or a BTCPay invoice reference if you paid in cryptocurrency, together with your tier) - could in aggregate reveal patterns about when you used the service, who you share with, and from which devices. We take this seriously and mitigate the risk through:

  • Every category of plaintext metadata has its retention stated in section 6. Most carry a maximum period, and the rest run until you delete the thing they describe, or your account. Nothing there is kept against your account once you delete it, with two exceptions: encrypted backups, whose residual copies persist for up to 90 days afterwards; and anything we are required to keep by law or a valid legal order. There used to be a third - your location and notes live in separate services from your account, and if one of them could not be reached at the moment you deleted, its rows stayed behind unidentifiable. That is no longer how it works: the erasure each service owes you is written down and retried until it completes, so an outage delays it rather than losing it. Where a row's limit is something other than a fixed number of days, it says so where it sits rather than leave this line to imply a clock it does not have
  • In Track, the event time your device recorded is coarsened to a day bucket; the time our servers received the row is not, as section 3.5 explains (Note sync timestamps are stored at full precision, as described in Section 3.6)
  • Track and Note content and product metadata are held in separate systems; your account (email) links both
  • We do not build user profiles, run behavioral analytics, or perform data mining on metadata

5.1 Circles: where you and we are joint controllers

When somebody puts you in a circle, two people are deciding things about your location. They decide that it should be collected at all, who else is in the circle and how long the history runs. We decide almost everything about how - the encryption, where it is stored, when it is deleted, what the apps will and will not do. Under GDPR Article 26 that makes us joint controllers with them for the location data in that circle, and Article 26 requires us to settle between us who answers for what and to tell you the essence of it. This is that.

We are responsible for telling you what is collected and why (this policy), keeping it secure, reporting a breach, deleting it when the retention periods in section 6 run out, and answering any request you make to us about the data we hold. We are also responsible for the tools that let you act without needing anyone's permission: leaving a circle, pausing, blurring your position, and seeing who is in it.

The person who added you is responsible for having a reason to collect your location at all, for telling you what that reason is if you ask, and for acting on it if you object to it - by removing you from the circle, or by accepting that you have left. They are also responsible for anything they exported and kept, which is on their device and beyond our reach entirely. If the circle is an ordinary household arrangement, the GDPR's household exemption may mean none of this lands on them; it is read narrowly, and section 1 of our Terms sets out where it may stop.

One consequence of the encryption is worth stating plainly. We hold no key to a circle, so if you ask us for a copy of your location data we can give you the encrypted records and the metadata around them, and we cannot give you the readable positions - those can only be produced by a device that holds the circle's key, which means your own device or another member's. This is not us declining. It is the same property that stops us reading it, and it cuts both ways.

Whatever this arrangement says, Article 26(3) lets you exercise your rights against either of us. You never have to work out whose responsibility something is before you ask. Write to privacy@mail.pingolin.com and we will do whatever is ours to do and tell you plainly what is not - and if what you want is simply for it to stop, leaving the circle does that immediately and needs nobody's agreement.

8. Your rights

8.1 Your rights in the EEA, the UK and Switzerland

Three laws, not one: the GDPR in the EEA, the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 in the United Kingdom, and the Federal Act on Data Protection in Switzerland. They differ in detail, and where they do we apply whichever is better for you rather than making you work out which one you are under. You have the right to:

  • Access: Request a copy of the personal data we hold about you
  • Rectification: Correct inaccurate personal data
  • Erasure: Request deletion of your personal data. One limit on that, and it is the one people ask about: your location entries in a circle are sealed with that circle's key, which we do not hold, so while your account exists we cannot pick your rows out of somebody else's circle and delete them on request. Two things do remove them - deleting your account, which erases them from every circle you were in, or leaving the circle and waiting out its retention period. Section 7 of our Terms sets out what leaving reaches and what it does not.
  • Portability: Receive your data in a structured, machine-readable format
  • Restriction: Request that we restrict processing of your data
  • Objection: Object to processing based on legitimate interest
  • Withdraw consent: Withdraw consent at any time where processing is based on consent

You also have the right to lodge a complaint with your local data protection authority - in the UK the Information Commissioner's Office, in Switzerland the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner, and in the EEA the authority for the country where you live or work. You do not have to raise it with us first, though we would rather you did so we can fix it.

10. Cookies and local storage

We use only strictly necessary cookies required for authentication and session management. These are HttpOnly, Secure, and SameSite=Lax. We do not use advertising cookies, tracking cookies, or third-party analytics scripts.

Our web apps also keep things in your browser's own storage, which this section covers as much as it covers cookies. Two mechanisms are in use. 12. Children's privacy

Pingolin is not directed to children, and the minimum age to create an account is 16. There is no consent that lowers it: if we become aware that an account holder is under 16 we delete the account and all its data, whatever any adult has agreed to.

Between 16 and 18 a parent or guardian must consent, and Section 5 of our Terms sets out the two ways that can work. In the first the minor holds their own account and keys and the parent simply consents; the parent cannot read what the account holds. In the second the parent creates and holds the account, and can therefore read everything in it. Those are genuinely different arrangements with different consequences for the minor's privacy, and the Terms describe both, along with what has to happen when the minor turns 18.

Be clear about the limits of that. We would rather say that plainly than claim a verification we do not perform. If you believe an account has been created by someone under 16, or by an adult misrepresenting their authority over a minor, contact us at privacy@mail.pingolin.com and we will act on it promptly.

13. Data breach notification

In the event of a security breach affecting personal data, we will notify affected users by email within 72 hours of confirming the breach - unless one of three things applies: a written request from law enforcement to hold off, which several breach-notification statutes permit; an intrusion we are still shutting down, where telling everyone before it is closed would make it worse; or our not yet knowing who is affected, in which case we will post a notice on this website inside the same 72 hours and email people as soon as we can identify them. Where any of them applies we will tell you there was a delay and why. That is stricter than the law requires of us: GDPR's 72-hour deadline (Article 33) applies to notifying the supervisory authority, while notification to you (Article 34) is required "without undue delay" where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to your rights. We will also notify the relevant supervisory authority within its own deadline, and comply with US state breach notification laws, including California Civil Code Section 1798.82 and equivalent statutes in other states where affected users reside.

Our notification will include the nature of the breach, the categories of data affected, the likely consequences, and the measures taken to address and mitigate the breach.

Due to our zero-knowledge architecture, a breach of our servers would not expose the contents of your encrypted data - only encrypted ciphertext and the limited plaintext metadata described in Section 3 (such as email addresses, IP addresses, and device metadata). Be precise about what that does and does not mean, because this sentence sits in the section that decides whether you get told: your SRP verifier, your key-derivation settings and your master key sealed under a password-derived key all live in the same database, so somebody who took a copy could try to guess your password offline and, if they succeeded, unseal what it protects. Your password's strength is part of this protection rather than an optional extra - and we will not treat "it was encrypted" as a reason to decide on our own that you need not be told. While this substantially limits the impact of any server breach, we treat any unauthorized access to metadata with the same urgency as a full data breach.
